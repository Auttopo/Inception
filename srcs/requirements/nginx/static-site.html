The Basics — Chapter 1

Mise en Place

N°01

Ingredients

🍞 Pizza dough

For 4 pizzas:

Bread flour or strong pizza flour: 600 g

Water: 390 g

Fine sea salt: 18 g

Instant dry yeast: 1.5 g

Olive oil: 10 g, optional

This gives approximately 65% hydration.

That means the dough contains water equal to about 65% of the flour's weight.

This is a useful level for a home pizza: wet enough to create a light, airy crust, but not so wet that it becomes impossible to handle.

Flour

If you can find it, use:

Italian 00 pizza flour

strong bread flour

pizza flour with approximately 11.5–13% protein

Don't obsess over the "00" designation. "00" mainly describes how finely the flour is milled. It does not automatically mean that every 00 flour is ideal for pizza.

For long fermentation, flour strength matters.

N°02

Tomato sauce

You don't need to cook the sauce.

For approximately 4 pizzas:

400 g canned peeled tomatoes

1–2 g fine sea salt

1 tablespoon extra-virgin olive oil

Optional: a small amount of dried oregano

Optional: 1 small garlic clove

Optional: a few basil leaves

For a classic pizza, I strongly recommend keeping the sauce simple.

The tomato should taste like tomato.

N°03

Cheese

The classic choice is mozzarella.

You can use:

Fresh mozzarella

Very delicious, but it contains a lot of water.

If using fresh mozzarella:

Drain it.

Tear it into pieces.

Place it on a plate.

Refrigerate it uncovered for several hours, or ideally overnight.

This allows excess moisture to escape.

Low-moisture mozzarella

This is actually extremely convenient for home ovens.

It melts beautifully and produces less water on your pizza.

Best combination

For a particularly good home pizza, try:

70% low-moisture mozzarella + 30% fresh mozzarella.

N°04

Toppings

For the simplest and most classic pizza:

Tomato sauce

Mozzarella

Fresh basil

Extra-virgin olive oil

You can add: mushrooms, onions, olives, roasted peppers, cooked sausage, ham, prosciutto, anchovies, chili flakes, Parmesan, pecorino, garlic, artichokes.

But there is an important rule:

Don't overload the pizza.

Pizza dough is relatively delicate.

Every additional topping contributes: weight, moisture, steam, cooling, cooking time.

A pizza covered with six different toppings can actually taste worse than a pizza with three excellent ingredients.

N°05

Equipment

Ideally: Large mixing bowl, Kitchen scale, Dough scraper, Plastic container or covered tray, Baking paper if necessary, Pizza peel, Pizza stone or pizza steel, Very hot oven.

Pizza steel

If you have one, use it.

A steel transfers heat extremely efficiently into the bottom of the pizza.

Pizza stone

Also excellent.

No stone or steel?

Don't worry.

You can use: a heavy baking tray, an upside-down baking sheet, a cast-iron pan.

An upside-down baking sheet placed near the top of a very hot oven can work surprisingly well.

Fermentation — Chapter 2

The Dough

N°06

The 48-hour schedule

Here's the overall plan.

Day 1: Morning or afternoon — Make dough. Let it ferment at room temperature. Divide into four balls. Refrigerate.

Day 2: Keep dough refrigerated. The slow fermentation develops flavor and structure.

Day 3: Remove dough from refrigerator approximately 2–4 hours before baking. Preheat oven aggressively. Shape pizza. Add toppings. Bake.

N°07

Making the dough

Step 1 — Measure everything

This is one of the rare situations where I strongly recommend using a kitchen scale.

Pizza dough is much easier to reproduce when measured by weight.

Put your mixing bowl on the scale.

Add 390 g water. Then add 1.5 g instant yeast. Mix briefly.

N°08

Add the flour

Add approximately 600 g flour.

Don't dump everything in blindly if you're new to handling wet dough.

You can reserve around 20–30 g of the flour initially and add it if necessary.

Mix with your hand, wooden spoon, or dough whisk until there is no large pocket of dry flour.

At this point, the dough will look rough. That's completely normal. It may look like a sticky, ugly mass. Don't panic. Pizza dough does not need to look beautiful immediately.

N°09

Rest the dough

Cover the bowl. Let it rest for approximately 15–20 minutes.

This short rest is called an autolyse-like rest, although because yeast and other ingredients are already present, it isn't a strict traditional autolyse.

Something useful happens during this period: the flour absorbs water. The dough begins becoming easier to work with.

N°10

Add the salt

Add 18 g salt. Work it into the dough. Pinch and fold the dough repeatedly.

At first it may feel like the salt is impossible to incorporate. Keep going. Eventually it becomes homogeneous.

N°11

Add olive oil

Add 10 g olive oil, if using. This is optional.

Traditional Neapolitan dough doesn't require oil.

However, in a home oven, a little oil can help with: browning, tenderness, handling, flavor.

If you want a more traditional Neapolitan-style dough, leave it out.

N°12

Kneading

You don't need to aggressively beat the dough for 20 minutes.

Instead, use a combination of: kneading + resting + folding. This is much easier.

Method: Grab one side of the dough. Stretch it upward. Fold it over itself. Rotate the bowl. Repeat.

Do this approximately 8–10 times. Then cover the dough and rest it for 10 minutes. Repeat this process another 1–2 times.

You'll notice something remarkable. The dough gradually changes. Initially: sticky, rough, weak. Later: smoother, elastic, stronger. This is gluten development.

N°13

What is gluten?

Flour contains proteins. When those proteins interact with water and mechanical movement, they form a network. That network is gluten.

Gluten provides structure. It allows the dough to stretch, trap fermentation gases, rise, hold its shape.

You don't want a completely rigid dough. You want a dough that is strong but extensible. That distinction is extremely important.

N°14

The windowpane test

Take a small piece of dough. Stretch it gently between your fingers. Try to make a thin membrane.

If you can stretch it until light passes through without it immediately tearing, your gluten development is good. This is called the windowpane test.

It doesn't have to be perfect. Pizza dough can continue developing during fermentation.

N°15

First fermentation

Put the dough into a lightly oiled container. Cover it. Leave it at room temperature for approximately 1–2 hours.

The goal isn't necessarily to double the dough. You are looking for signs of fermentation: slight expansion, bubbles, smoother texture, increased elasticity, pleasant fermented aroma.

N°16

Divide the dough

Turn the dough onto a lightly floured work surface. Divide it into 4 equal pieces.

With 600 g flour, your dough will weigh approximately 1 kg total after adding water, salt, yeast, and oil. Each dough ball will therefore be approximately 250 g. This is a great size for a 28–30 cm pizza.

N°17

Forming dough balls

Take one piece. Fold the edges toward the bottom. Rotate it. Continue until you have created a smooth upper surface.

Then gently drag the dough ball across the work surface toward yourself. The friction between the dough and counter creates surface tension.

You want: smooth top and sealed underside. Don't completely crush the dough. You are building a little package of gluten.

N°18

Refrigeration

Place the four dough balls into a covered container. Leave enough space between them. Cover tightly.

Refrigerate at approximately 3–5°C / 37–41°F. Leave them for 36–48 hours. This is where the magic happens.

N°19

Why ferment for two days?

Fermentation isn't simply about making dough rise. It also changes flavor and texture.

During fermentation, yeast consumes sugars and produces: carbon dioxide, alcohol, flavor compounds. Meanwhile, enzymes in the flour modify the dough.

Long fermentation can produce: more complex flavor, better browning, improved extensibility, lighter texture, more aromatic crust.

This is one reason a pizza made with dough fermented for two days can taste dramatically more interesting than one made immediately after mixing.

Toppings — Chapter 3

Sauce & Cheese

N°20

Making the tomato sauce

While your dough is fermenting, make your sauce.

Open 400 g canned peeled tomatoes. If they're whole tomatoes, crush them with your hands. Remove any tough stem pieces.

Add 1–2 g salt. Add 1 tablespoon olive oil. That's enough.

You can blend the tomatoes, but I actually prefer crushing them by hand. This produces a slightly rustic texture.

N°21

Should you cook the sauce?

For this style of pizza: No.

The sauce cooks on the pizza. Using raw tomatoes gives you: fresh flavor, acidity, brightness, less concentrated sweetness.

However, if you're making pizza in a relatively cool home oven and baking for 8–12 minutes, a cooked sauce can work better. More on that later.

N°22

Preparing the cheese

Take your mozzarella. Cut or tear it into small pieces. Avoid creating giant chunks.

A giant piece of mozzarella can release a lot of water in one location. Small pieces distribute the cheese more evenly.

If using fresh mozzarella, drain it thoroughly. This is one of the most important moisture-control steps.

The Oven — Chapter 4

Bake Day

N°23

Baking day

This is where many homemade pizzas fail.

The dough might be excellent. The sauce might be excellent. The cheese might be excellent.

But if the oven isn't hot enough, the pizza becomes: soft, pale, wet, and bready.

We want: very high heat.

N°24

Preheat the oven

Put your pizza steel or stone inside the oven. Place it relatively high in the oven.

Then preheat to the maximum temperature your oven safely reaches. For many domestic ovens, that's 250–300°C / 480–570°F.

If your oven has a broiler/grill function, that's extremely useful.

Preheat for at least 45–60 minutes. A stone or steel needs time to become thoroughly hot.

Don't assume that because the oven says "preheated," the cooking surface is fully ready.

N°25

Why the oven needs to be so hot

A professional pizza oven can reach temperatures far above a normal home oven.

A Neapolitan pizza may cook in roughly 60–120 seconds. A home oven might require 4–8 minutes, depending on temperature and setup.

The hotter the oven, the faster the pizza cooks.

Fast cooking gives you the ideal combination: crisp bottom, airy crust, melted cheese, browned toppings, moist center.

Slow cooking tends to dry the crust before you get good browning.

N°26

Remove dough from refrigerator

Take out one dough ball approximately 2–4 hours before baking. The exact timing depends on your room temperature.

At this stage, the dough should become: softer, more relaxed, slightly larger, easier to stretch.

Don't immediately attack a cold dough ball. Cold dough is much harder to stretch.

N°27

The most important pizza-shaping rule

Do NOT use a rolling pin.

A rolling pin pushes all the fermentation gas out of the dough. Those gas bubbles are valuable. They are responsible for the beautiful airy crust.

Instead: stretch the dough gently with your hands.

N°28

Shaping the pizza

Generously flour your work surface. Rice flour can be particularly useful because it doesn't absorb water as quickly as wheat flour.

Take one dough ball. Place it on the flour. Dust the top lightly. Press your fingertips into the center. Work outward.

Leave approximately 2–3 cm / 1 inch around the edge untouched. This becomes your crust.

N°29

Why leave the edge untouched?

Because the edge contains fermentation gases. When heated, those gases expand. The gluten network stretches. Water turns into steam.

The result: puffy crust.

If you aggressively flatten the edge, you remove the very thing you're trying to create.

N°30

Continue stretching

Once you've flattened the center, gently lift the dough. Use the backs of your hands or fingertips. Rotate the dough. Let gravity help. Stretch gradually.

You're aiming for approximately 28–30 cm / 11–12 inches.

Don't force it. If the dough keeps shrinking: stop. Let it rest for 5–10 minutes. Then continue. Shrinking usually means the gluten is resisting. Resting allows the gluten to relax.

N°31

How thin should the center be?

The center should be quite thin. You should almost be able to see light through parts of it. But don't create holes.

A thin center cooks quickly and creates the characteristic pizza texture.

N°32

Transfer to the peel

Dust your pizza peel with a small amount of flour. Alternatively, use semolina or rice flour.

Place the stretched dough onto the peel. Work quickly now. Once the dough touches the peel, it can begin sticking.

N°33

Sauce

Add approximately 60–80 g tomato sauce. Don't pour half the jar onto the pizza.

Use the back of a spoon. Start near the center. Move outward in a spiral. Stop before the crust.

You want a thin, even layer.

N°34

Cheese

Add approximately 80–100 g mozzarella. Distribute it evenly. Don't completely cover every millimeter of sauce.

Some tomato should remain visible. Some exposed sauce will become concentrated and flavorful.

N°35

Basil

Add a few basil leaves. You can put some under the cheese and some on top.

At extremely high temperatures, basil can burn quickly. An alternative is to add fresh basil after baking.

N°36

Olive oil

Finish with a small drizzle: 1–2 teaspoons extra-virgin olive oil. Don't drown the pizza. A little goes a long way.

N°37

The launch

This is the moment that makes beginners nervous.

Shake the peel gently. The pizza should move.

If it doesn't: DO NOT PUT IT IN THE OVEN YET.

A stuck pizza is difficult to recover. Lift the edge of the dough carefully and throw a little flour underneath the sticky section. Then shake again.

N°38

Launching onto the stone

Open the oven. Position the peel close to the baking surface. Then make a quick forward-and-back movement.

The dough should slide off. Pull the peel away. The pizza remains on the hot surface. Immediately close the oven.

N°39

Baking

At maximum home-oven temperature: approximately 4–7 minutes is a reasonable starting range.

But don't blindly follow the clock. Watch the pizza. You're looking for: bubbling cheese, browned spots, puffed crust, darkened underside, blistering around the rim, fully cooked center.

N°40

Using the broiler/grill

This is one of my favorite home-pizza tricks.

If the bottom is cooked but the top isn't sufficiently browned: turn on the broiler/grill.

Move the pizza closer to the upper heating element. Watch it constantly. It may go from "not browned enough" to "burned" very quickly. Sometimes only 30–60 seconds can make a huge difference.

N°41

Don't cut immediately

Remove the pizza. Place it on a cutting board. Wait approximately 30–60 seconds. Then cut.

Why? Because the cheese and internal moisture are still extremely hot. Cutting immediately can cause everything to slide around.

N°42

Final finishing

After baking, add: fresh basil, extra-virgin olive oil, Parmesan, chili flakes, freshly ground black pepper — depending on the pizza.

For a Margherita, I like: basil + tiny drizzle of olive oil. That's it.

The Recipes — Chapter 5

The Pizzas

N°43

Your first pizza: Margherita

Ingredients for one pizza: 1 × 250 g dough ball, 60–80 g tomato sauce, 80–100 g mozzarella, 3–5 basil leaves, 1–2 teaspoons extra-virgin olive oil.

Procedure: Preheat oven and stone/steel thoroughly. Bring dough to room temperature. Flour the work surface. Remove dough from container. Gently press the center. Preserve the outer rim. Stretch to approximately 28–30 cm. Transfer to floured peel. Add tomato sauce. Add mozzarella. Add basil. Drizzle olive oil. Shake peel to ensure mobility. Launch. Bake until crust is puffed and browned. Use broiler if necessary. Remove. Rest briefly. Add finishing basil/olive oil. Slice. Eat immediately.

N°44

Pizza #2 — Pepperoni

For one pizza: 250 g dough, 60–70 g tomato sauce, 90 g mozzarella, 50–70 g pepperoni, chili flakes, olive oil.

Put the pepperoni on top of the cheese. The fat will render during baking.

If you want crispy pepperoni cups, arrange slices so their edges can curl. Don't use too much. A massive amount of pepperoni creates an oily pizza.

N°45

Pizza #3 — Mushroom

Use: tomato sauce, mozzarella, mushrooms, Parmesan, olive oil, thyme.

Important: Raw mushrooms contain a lot of water. If you put a huge pile of raw mushrooms on your pizza, you'll probably get wet pizza.

Instead: Slice mushrooms thinly. Cook them briefly in a hot pan. Allow them to release moisture. Cool them. Put them on the pizza. This produces much better results.

N°46

Pizza #4 — Prosciutto and arugula

Bake the pizza with: tomato, mozzarella, olive oil.

After baking, add: thinly sliced prosciutto, arugula, Parmesan, olive oil.

The key is that the prosciutto and arugula don't necessarily need to be cooked. Their fresh texture contrasts beautifully with the hot pizza.

N°47

Pizza #5 — Four cheese

Use approximately: mozzarella, Gorgonzola, Parmesan, Fontina.

Don't cover the pizza with enormous quantities of cheese. A four-cheese pizza should still have balance. The cheeses should complement each other rather than creating a thick layer of melted fat.

N°48

Pizza #6 — Garlic and herb

Try: mozzarella, roasted garlic, Parmesan, oregano, basil, olive oil.

Roasted garlic is much sweeter and softer than raw garlic. You can roast whole garlic cloves until tender, then mash them into a paste.

N°49

Pizza #7 — Spicy pizza

Use: tomato sauce, mozzarella, spicy sausage, chili flakes, red onion, hot honey after baking.

The combination of heat + salt + acidity + sweetness is fantastic.

The Craft — Chapter 6

Troubleshooting & Science

N°50

The science of crispy pizza

A pizza crust becomes crisp largely because water leaves the dough during baking. The surface becomes hotter and drier.

The Maillard reaction then creates complex browned flavors. This reaction is responsible for many of the aromas associated with: bread, roasted meat, toasted nuts, baked potatoes, pizza crust.

High heat accelerates browning.

N°51

Why your crust might be pale

If your crust is pale, possible causes include:

Oven isn't hot enough — Most common.

Stone isn't preheated enough — Also common.

Too much moisture — Too much sauce or wet cheese prevents rapid browning.

Dough lacks available sugars — Very long fermentation or certain flour conditions can affect browning.

Pizza is too thick — A thicker pizza takes longer to dry and brown.

N°52

Why your pizza might be soggy

Potential causes: too much tomato sauce, wet mozzarella, too many toppings, insufficient oven heat, stone not hot enough, pizza too thick, underbaking, toppings releasing water.

The solution isn't necessarily "cook it longer." If the oven is weak, cooking longer may produce: dry crust + wet center.

The better solution is often: more heat.

N°53

Why your dough sticks to the peel

Possible reasons: Too much time on the peel — prepare your pizza and launch it quickly. Not enough flour — use a little more. Dough is extremely wet — reduce hydration slightly. Wet toppings — sauce can leak moisture into the dough.

Peel material: Wooden peels are often easier for launching. Metal peels are excellent for retrieving pizza but can sometimes make launching more difficult because dough sticks quickly.

N°54

Why your dough tears

Possible reasons: insufficient gluten development, dough too wet, dough over-fermented, aggressive stretching, insufficient resting, weak flour.

Try letting the dough rest for 10–15 minutes. Then stretch again gently.

N°55

Why the dough keeps shrinking

This is usually gluten tension. You stretch it. It shrinks. You stretch it. It shrinks again.

Don't fight it. Cover it and wait 10–20 minutes. Then try again. The dough will usually become much more cooperative.

N°56

Why your crust isn't airy

This can happen because you: rolled the dough, pressed the rim flat, over-handled the dough, fermented too little, used too much yeast and rushed fermentation, didn't allow the dough to warm before shaping.

The most important thing: Protect the gas. The bubbles created during fermentation are your friends.

N°57

Why your pizza is too chewy

Some chew is desirable. But if it's excessively chewy: dough may be under-fermented, flour may be extremely strong, hydration may be too low, pizza may be undercooked, dough may have been stretched too thickly.

Try slightly longer fermentation and/or slightly higher hydration.

N°58

Why your pizza is too soft

If you want a crispier base: preheat longer, use a steel, reduce toppings, reduce sauce, bake at maximum temperature, position steel appropriately, allow the oven to recover between pizzas.

N°59

Between pizzas

This is important. When you open the oven and remove a pizza, the oven temperature can drop significantly.

Wait a little before launching the next pizza. Allow the stone or steel to recover.

This is particularly important if you're making 4 pizzas in succession.

N°60

Don't make four pizzas at once

For most home ovens: one pizza at a time is best.

The process becomes: Shape pizza. Top pizza. Launch. Bake. Remove. Let oven recover. Shape next pizza. Repeat.

N°61

A fantastic home-oven trick

If your oven has a top broiler: Place your steel or stone relatively close to the top heating element. Preheat thoroughly. Bake the pizza on the steel. Then finish with the broiler.

This can imitate the intense top heat of a pizza oven surprisingly well. The bottom receives intense heat from the steel. The top receives intense radiant heat from the broiler. That's exactly what we're looking for.

N°62

A second method: cast-iron pizza

If you don't have a pizza stone or steel, try a cast-iron pan.

Put your dough into a lightly oiled cast-iron skillet. Press it gently outward. Let it rest. Add sauce and toppings. Bake at maximum temperature.

This produces a thicker, deeply browned crust. It's not Neapolitan pizza. But it can be absolutely fantastic.

N°63

Detroit-style variation

For something completely different: Use a rectangular metal pan. Generously oil it. Press dough into the pan. Let it ferment. Add cheese all the way to the edges. Add toppings. Bake.

The cheese touching the pan caramelizes against the metal. The result is: crispy, cheesy edges + fluffy interior.

N°64

Pan pizza variation

For a softer, thicker pizza: Increase the dough thickness. Use approximately 300–350 g dough per 25 cm pan. Oil the pan generously. Let the dough rise inside the pan. Add toppings. Bake until deeply browned.

This is much more forgiving than thin pizza.

N°65

The importance of salt

Salt isn't just there to make the pizza taste salty. It also affects: gluten development, fermentation, flavor, dough strength.

Too little salt can make pizza taste flat. Too much can slow fermentation dramatically.

The approximately 3% salt level in this recipe is a good starting point.

N°66

The importance of water

Water controls dough texture. More water generally means: more extensible + potentially lighter, but also: stickier + harder to handle.

Less water generally means: easier handling + firmer dough, but potentially: denser crust.

If you're new to pizza, don't immediately chase extremely high hydration. A manageable dough is much more useful than an impressive number.

N°67

The importance of fermentation temperature

Temperature changes fermentation speed dramatically. Warm: fast fermentation. Cool: slow fermentation. Cold: very slow fermentation.

That's why refrigeration is so useful. It gives you control.

N°68

If you want a 24-hour dough

Use the same basic recipe. Instead of refrigerating for 48 hours, refrigerate approximately 18–24 hours.

Then bring it to room temperature for several hours before baking.

The flavor will be somewhat less developed than the 48-hour version, but it can still be excellent.

N°69

If you want a same-day pizza

Use a little more yeast. For example: 600 g flour, 390 g water, 18 g salt, approximately 4–6 g instant yeast.

Mix. Knead. Let ferment at room temperature. Divide. Ball. Allow the dough balls to rise. Bake the same day.

The pizza can still be very good. The tradeoff is that you'll get less of the flavor associated with slow fermentation.

N°70

If you want 72-hour fermentation

You can extend refrigeration further, but you'll generally want to reduce yeast.

A 72-hour dough can become extremely flavorful and extensible. However, eventually the dough can become: weak, sticky, overly acidic, difficult to shape.

Longer does not automatically mean better.

N°71

Don't chase fermentation records

A pizza fermented for 72 hours isn't automatically better than one fermented for 48 hours.

The ideal fermentation depends on: flour, yeast, temperature, hydration, salt, refrigerator temperature, dough quantity, handling.

Pizza is a system. Change one variable and the others can behave differently.

After Baking — Chapter 7

Storage & Master Card

N°72

How to store leftover pizza

If you somehow have leftovers: Let them cool. Refrigerate in a sealed container. Eat within approximately 3–4 days for best quality.

N°73

Best way to reheat pizza

Don't use the microwave if you want a crispy crust.

Skillet method: Put pizza in a dry skillet. Heat over medium-low. Cover. Heat until the bottom becomes crisp. Add a few drops of water to the pan away from the pizza. Cover again briefly.

The steam warms the toppings while the pan crisps the bottom. It's excellent.

N°74

Oven reheating

Preheat oven to approximately 200–230°C / 400–450°F. Put pizza directly on a rack or hot tray. Heat for 5–8 minutes.

The exact time depends on thickness.

N°75

Freezing pizza dough

You can freeze dough. After forming dough balls: Place each ball in a sealed container. Freeze. When needed, transfer to refrigerator. Allow it to thaw slowly. Bring to room temperature. Shape gently.

Frozen dough may not behave exactly like freshly fermented dough, but it can work very well.

N°76

Freezing baked pizza

You can also freeze cooked slices. Separate slices with baking paper. Place into a freezer-safe container or bag. Reheat directly from frozen in a hot oven.

This is considerably better than letting them sit in the refrigerator for a week.

N°77

A perfect pizza assembly formula

For a 30 cm pizza, a useful starting point is approximately: Dough 250 g, Sauce 60–80 g, Cheese 80–100 g, Additional toppings usually 30–80 g depending on the topping, Olive oil 5–10 g.

That's enough.

N°78

The "less is more" principle

Imagine the pizza as a balance between: dough + tomato + cheese + toppings.

If one ingredient dominates, the pizza becomes unbalanced. For example: Too much sauce — wet. Too much cheese — heavy. Too much meat — greasy and salty. Too many vegetables — wet. Too much flour on the peel — bitter and dusty. Too much olive oil — greasy.

The best pizzas often look surprisingly simple.

N°79

Ingredient quality matters

You don't necessarily need expensive ingredients. But use good basics.

Tomatoes: Look for canned tomatoes with good flavor and relatively simple ingredients.

Olive oil: A flavorful extra-virgin olive oil is noticeable when used after baking.

Mozzarella: Fresh, good-quality mozzarella makes a major difference.

Flour: A flour suitable for pizza and long fermentation will make your life easier.

Salt: Fine sea salt is perfect.

N°80

The ultimate Margherita

If I were making the pizza from this recipe for myself, I'd keep it extremely simple: Dough 250 g, Sauce 70 g good canned tomatoes, Cheese 90 g mozzarella, Basil 4 leaves, Oil 5 g excellent extra-virgin olive oil.

Then: very hot oven, very hot steel, short bake, finish with basil and olive oil. That's enough.

N°81

A complete timeline

Day 1 — 14:00: Mix 390 g water, 1.5 g yeast, 600 g flour. Rest 20 minutes. Add 18 g salt, 10 g olive oil if desired. Knead/fold.

15:00 — Begin bulk fermentation.

16:30 — Divide into four pieces. Form four dough balls.

17:00 — Cover and refrigerate.

Day 2 — Leave refrigerated. Don't disturb them unnecessarily.

Day 3 — approximately 15:00: Remove dough from refrigerator. Keep covered.

17:00 — Begin preheating oven and pizza steel/stone. Maximum temperature.

18:00 — Shape first pizza. Add sauce. Add cheese. Add toppings. Launch. Bake.

18:10 — First pizza is ready. Eat.

18:15 — Shape second pizza.

18:25 — Bake second pizza. Continue.

N°82

The most important lessons

If you remember only ten things from this entire recipe, remember these:

1. Use a scale.

2. Give the dough time.

3. Don't use a rolling pin.

4. Protect the outer rim.

5. Don't overload toppings.

6. Drain wet mozzarella.

7. Preheat your stone or steel for a long time.

8. Use the hottest oven setting available.

9. Launch quickly after topping.

10. Eat the pizza immediately.

Final "Master Recipe" Card

Dough (for 4 pizzas): 600 g strong flour, 390 g water, 18 g salt, 1.5 g instant yeast, 10 g olive oil (optional).

Method: Mix water and yeast. Add flour. Mix until combined. Rest 15–20 minutes. Add salt. Add optional olive oil. Knead/fold until dough becomes smooth and elastic. Ferment 1–2 hours at room temperature. Divide into 4 × approximately 250 g. Form tight dough balls. Refrigerate 36–48 hours. Remove 2–4 hours before baking. Shape gently without rolling. Preserve the outer rim.

Sauce: 400 g canned tomatoes, 1–2 g salt, olive oil, optional basil. Crush together. Don't cook.

Topping per pizza: 60–80 g sauce, 80–100 g mozzarella, basil, olive oil, additional toppings as desired.

Baking: Put stone/steel in oven. Preheat at maximum temperature for 45–60+ minutes. Stretch dough to 28–30 cm. Place on peel. Add sauce. Add cheese. Add toppings. Launch immediately. Bake approximately 4–7 minutes in a very hot home oven. Finish under broiler if necessary. Remove. Add fresh basil/olive oil. Rest briefly. Slice. Eat.

And the single biggest secret...

The best homemade pizza isn't produced by one magical ingredient.

It's the combination of: good flour + correct hydration + enough fermentation + gentle shaping + restrained toppings + a genuinely hot cooking surface + fast baking.

If you get those seven things right, you can make pizza at home that is dramatically better than most ordinary homemade pizza.
